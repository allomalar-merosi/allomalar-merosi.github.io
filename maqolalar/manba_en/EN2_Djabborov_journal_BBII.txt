ABSTRACT: //Purpose// – When a state-owned bank is sold to a foreign strategic investor, individual stability indicators often move in opposite directions: profitability may rise while recognized non-performing loans increase. This paper develops an integral bank stability index (BBII) that combines a CAMELS-type composite rating with the Z-score and applies it to Ipoteka Bank, the first state-owned bank in Uzbekistan sold to a foreign strategic investor. //Design/methodology/approach// – CAMELS components are scored on a 1–5 scale using thresholds calibrated to Uzbek prudential requirements and banking-system characteristics; the Z-score is computed with a time-varying capital ratio and a full-period standard deviation of returns. The two are normalized and combined with weights of 0.6 and 0.4. The index is computed from the bank's audited IFRS consolidated statements for 2019–2025 and Central Bank of Uzbekistan bank-level statistics, and sensitivity analysis is performed on weights, the Z-score benchmark, the accounting basis and the asset quality score. //Findings// – The index declines from 0.556 in 2019 to 0.455–0.489 in the years before the sale, falls to 0.255 in the integration year 2023, when the share of credit-impaired loans rose from 7.0 to 18.7 per cent and the bank lost UZS 1.6 trillion, and recovers to 0.562 in 2025, its highest value over the period. The J-shaped trajectory is robust to all parameter choices tested. The level of the index is highly sensitive to the accounting basis (0.562 under IFRS versus 0.720 with national-standard asset quality and liquidity ratios), and asset quality is the binding constraint: each one-notch improvement in the asset quality score raises the index by 0.0375. //Originality/value// – The index provides a single, transparent measure that reconciles conflicting signals during post-privatization integration and can be computed from public disclosures, supporting supervisors and policymakers in emerging markets that are privatizing state banks.

# 1. Introduction

Since 2020 Uzbekistan has pursued a strategy to transform a banking system dominated by the state. At the time the 2020–2025 banking reform strategy was adopted, 13 of 31 banks were state-owned and controlled about 85 per cent of banking assets; the strategy set targets of raising the share of banks without state participation from 15 to 60 per cent of assets and attracting at least three foreign strategic investors (Presidential Decree No. PF-5992, 2020; GRATA International, 2020). The first and so far the only transaction of this kind was completed on 13 June 2023, when Hungary's OTP Bank acquired 73.71 per cent of Ipoteka Bank (Ipoteka-bank, 2023). The price was not officially disclosed; press reports put it at about USD 324 million (bne IntelliNews, 2023). OTP Bank added USD 70 million of capital in December 2023 (Kun.uz, 2023); after the International Finance Corporation (IFC) converted a loan into equity in May 2026, the shareholders are OTP Bank (72.89 per cent), the Ministry of Economy and Finance (17.49 per cent) and IFC (8.69 per cent) (Ipoteka bank OTP Group, 2026).

The bank's post-acquisition indicators send conflicting signals. According to OTP Group, Ipoteka Bank was the only group member to report a loss in 2023 (Telex, 2024). The bank's audited IFRS statements show a net loss of UZS 1,616 billion in 2023, after loan-loss provisions of UZS 4,275 billion, followed by net profits of UZS 1,287 billion in 2024 and UZS 2,047 billion in 2025, when return on equity (ROE) reached 28.1 per cent (Ipoteka Bank, 2020–2026). Yet credit-impaired (Stage 3) loans still made up 19.7 per cent of the gross portfolio at end-2025. Under national accounting standards, the bank reported a loss of UZS 240.2 billion for 2024 (UzDaily, 2025), and its NPL ratio rose to 10.2 per cent in 2024 before falling to 3.5 per cent in 2025 (Central Bank of the Republic of Uzbekistan [CBU], 2026). Has the bank become more or less stable? Individual ratios cannot answer this question. This paper proposes an integral bank stability index (BBII) that combines a CAMELS-type composite rating, which captures the bank's current condition across six areas, with the Z-score, which measures distance to insolvency. The index is computed from public data, is bounded between 0 and 1, and has interpretable thresholds. Applied to Ipoteka Bank, it shows a J-shaped trajectory: stability was already eroding before the sale, collapsed in the integration year as hidden asset-quality problems were recognized, and then recovered to its highest level of the period.

The paper contributes to three strands of literature: the measurement of bank stability with rating systems and the Z-score (Lopez, 1999; Lepetit & Strobel, 2013); the effects of foreign acquisition and privatization on bank performance in transition economies (Bonin et al., 2005; Havrylchyk & Jurzyk, 2011); and the banking reform literature on Central Asia, where empirical work on post-privatization performance is scarce.

Section 2 reviews the literature, Section 3 describes the index and data, Section 4 presents results and sensitivity analysis, Section 5 discusses implications and limitations, and Section 6 concludes.

# 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Bank stability and its measurement

Bank stability is rooted in the fragility of maturity transformation: Diamond and Dybvig (1983) show that self-fulfilling runs can bring down a solvent bank, and Minsky (1986) argues that periods of stability breed risk-taking that later produces instability. Measurement approaches fall into four groups (Saunders & Cornett, 2018): financial ratios; supervisory rating systems; frontier efficiency methods; and composite risk measures. The CAMELS rating system, introduced in the United States in 1979 and extended with a sensitivity component in 1996, rates capital adequacy, asset quality, management, earnings, liquidity and sensitivity to market risk on a 1–5 scale. Lopez (1999) shows that CAMELS ratings are informative about bank conditions but that their information content decays quickly, while Sahajwala and Van den Bergh (2000) classify supervisory rating and early warning systems. The IMF's Financial Soundness Indicators (FSI) provide a standardized system-level analogue (IMF, 2025).

The Z-score, derived from Roy's (1952) safety-first principle and applied to banks by Boyd and Graham (1986), measures how many standard deviations returns must fall to exhaust equity. Lepetit and Strobel (2013) compare ways of computing time-varying Z-scores and recommend combining current capital ratios with full-sample means and standard deviations of ROA. Altman's (1968) Z-model is widely used for non-financial firms but is not designed for banks. Composite indicators require transparent normalization, weighting and sensitivity analysis (OECD & JRC, 2008; Saisana et al., 2005).

## 2.2. Foreign ownership, privatization and bank performance

Agency theory (Jensen & Meckling, 1976) and the corporate governance literature (Shleifer & Vishny, 1997) imply that a large private owner improves monitoring relative to diffuse or political ownership. La Porta et al. (2002) find that government ownership of banks is associated with slower financial development, and Sapienza (2004) documents politically driven lending by state banks. In transition economies, Bonin et al. (2005) show that foreign-owned banks are the most cost-efficient and that sales to strategic foreign investors outperform voucher privatization, and Fries and Taci (2005) obtain similar results for 15 post-communist countries. Berger et al. (2005) distinguish static, selection and dynamic effects of ownership, and Havrylchyk and Jurzyk (2011) show that the superior performance of acquired foreign banks is partly inherited and that profitability initially declines after acquisition. Megginson (2005) concludes that privatization generally improves performance but that outcomes depend on method and institutions.

Foreign ownership also shapes resilience. De Haas and van Lelyveld (2006) find that foreign subsidiaries in Central and Eastern Europe (CEE) stabilized credit during local crises, but during the global financial crisis multinational banks' subsidiaries reduced lending more than domestic banks (de Haas & van Lelyveld, 2014; Cull & Martínez Pería, 2013). Laeven and Levine (2009) show that the effect of regulation on risk depends on ownership structure. Foreign bank shares in CEE rose from about 10 per cent in 1995 to 77 per cent in 2008 (Prague Economic Papers, 2021), and above 80 per cent in the region's EU member states by 2009 (Claessens & van Horen, 2012).

The literature thus suggests that stability after a foreign acquisition may follow a non-linear path, but it offers no simple, public-data measure that reconciles the conflicting signals typical of the integration period. This paper fills that gap for an emerging market in the early stage of bank privatization.

## 2.3. Banking reform in Uzbekistan

Uzbekistan's banking system grew rapidly after the 2017 liberalization. According to the Central Bank, total assets rose from UZS 272.7 trillion at end-2019 to UZS 924.8 trillion at end-2025, deposits grew 4.6-fold and the loan-to-deposit ratio fell from 232 to 145 per cent; the capital adequacy ratio has been stable at 17–18 per cent since 2020 (CBU, 2026). The share of banks with state participation in total assets declined from 84.4 to 63.5 per cent over the same period, largely because private banks grew faster rather than because of privatization; the number of such banks fell from 13 to 9. State banks remain dependent on government funding – their loan-to-deposit ratio stood at 190 per cent at end-2025, compared with 98 per cent for other banks – and their reported NPL ratio (3.2 per cent) exceeds that of other banks (2.6 per cent) (CBU, 2026). The IMF has noted that NPLs may be under-reported (IMF, 2025). Privatization deadlines for Asakabank, Uzpromstroybank and Aloqabank were postponed, and the updated Uzbekistan–2030 strategy announced that no state bank would be privatized in 2026. In this setting, evidence on the first foreign acquisition is directly relevant to the design of future transactions.

# 3. Methodology and data

## 3.1. CAMELS component scoring

Each CAMELS component is proxied by one publicly observable ratio and scored from 1 (best) to 5 (worst) using thresholds calibrated to Uzbek conditions (Table 1). The capital thresholds are anchored on the national minimum total capital adequacy ratio of 13 per cent; the liquidity thresholds reflect the high loan-to-deposit ratios of the Uzbek system (173 per cent overall and 237 per cent for state banks at end-2024; CBU, 2026); management quality is proxied by the cost-to-income ratio, a common practice when supervisory assessments are unavailable.

TABLE 1: CAMELS components, proxies and scoring thresholds ## Author's elaboration based on Lopez (1999), Sahajwala and Van den Bergh (2000) and national prudential requirements.
| Component | Proxy | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
| C – Capital | Capital adequacy ratio, % | ≥ 18 | 15–18 | 13–15 | 10–13 | < 10 |
| A – Asset quality | Stage 3 (credit-impaired) loan ratio, % | < 2 | 2–4 | 4–6 | 6–10 | ≥ 10 |
| M – Management | Cost-to-income ratio, % | < 40 | 40–50 | 50–60 | 60–70 | ≥ 70 |
| E – Earnings | ROA, % | ≥ 2.5 | 1.5–2.5 | 0.5–1.5 | 0–0.5 | < 0 |
| L – Liquidity | Loans-to-deposits, % | < 100 | 100–130 | 130–170 | 170–220 | ≥ 220 |
| S – Sensitivity | Open FX position to capital, % | < 5 | 5–10 | 10–15 | 15–20 | ≥ 20 |

The composite score weights capital and asset quality most heavily:

$$ CAMELS_t = 0.25·C + 0.25·A + 0.10·M + 0.15·E + 0.15·L + 0.10·S (1)

## 3.2. Z-score

Following Lepetit and Strobel (2013), the Z-score combines current ROA and the current equity-to-assets ratio with the full-period standard deviation of ROA:

$$ Z_t = (ROA_t + E/A_t) / σ(ROA) (2)

## 3.3. Integral bank stability index

The integral index normalizes both measures to the unit interval and combines them:

$$ BBII_t = w_1 · (5 – CAMELS_t) / 4 + w_2 · min(Z_t / Z*, 1) (3)

with w_1 = 0.6, w_2 = 0.4 and a benchmark Z* = 15. Values above 0.75 indicate high stability, 0.5–0.75 satisfactory stability and below 0.5 low stability. The CAMELS component receives the larger weight because it captures more dimensions of bank condition; the Z-score adds forward-looking information about insolvency risk and earnings volatility.

## 3.4. Data

The analysis covers 2019–2025 and is based on the bank's audited IFRS consolidated financial statements (Ipoteka Bank, 2020–2026). Capital adequacy is the regulatory total capital ratio under CBU rules, disclosed in the capital management note; asset quality is the share of Stage 3 loans in gross loans; the management proxy is operating expenses divided by total operating income (net interest, net fee, trading and other income); ROA and ROE use average assets and equity; liquidity is net loans divided by customer accounts; and sensitivity is the sum of absolute net on-balance-sheet positions in foreign currencies divided by equity. Figures for 2022 are the restated values reported in the 2023 statements. For comparison, the index is also computed with the national-standard NPL and loan-to-deposit ratios published by the CBU (2026) for each bank.

The components moved in different directions. Capital weakened after the large 2019 state recapitalization, reaching 14.4 per cent in 2021 – only 1.4 percentage points above the minimum – and rose to 19.8 per cent in 2025. Asset quality deteriorated throughout and has received the worst score since 2023: in the integration year the existing portfolio was reclassified under the new owner's methodology and credit-impaired loans more than tripled, from UZS 2.4 trillion to UZS 7.5 trillion. Cost efficiency was strong until 2024 but weakened in 2025 as integration and technology costs rose. Earnings received the worst score in 2023 and the best in 2024–2025. Liquidity remained weak because a large share of funding consists of targeted Ministry of Finance loans rather than customer accounts, though it improved from 2024. Sensitivity deteriorated sharply in 2019 and 2023, when large equity changes (recapitalization, loss) temporarily pushed the FX position outside prudent bounds. The composite rating reached its worst level (3.85) in 2023 and improved to 2.65 in 2025.

The BBII traces a J-shaped path. It fell from 0.556 in 2019 to 0.455–0.489 in 2021–2022, showing that the bank's stability was already eroding before the sale as its capital buffer thinned and impaired loans accumulated. In the integration year the index collapsed to 0.255, returned to its pre-sale level in 2024 (0.497) and reached 0.562 in 2025, the highest value of the period. The pattern is consistent with the finding of Havrylchyk and Jurzyk (2011) that acquired banks' profitability first declines and then recovers, and with the interpretation that asset revaluation under group standards and IFRS 9 (IASB, 2014) temporarily worsens reported indicators.

Three factors keep the level of the index low. First, the asset quality score: impaired loans are concentrated in legacy business lending – at end-2025, 79 per cent of small business loans and 36 per cent of corporate loans were in Stage 3, against 2.8 per cent of mortgages. Second, the Z-score: the 2023 loss raised the volatility of returns from 0.50 percentage points before the sale to 2.42 points over the full period, so a single loss depresses the index for years. Third, liquidity: loans still exceed customer accounts by 92 per cent.

The choice of accounting basis matters greatly for asset quality (Figure 2). The national-standard NPL ratio rose to 10.2 per cent in 2024 and fell to 3.5 per cent in 2025, while the IFRS Stage 3 ratio stayed near 20 per cent. The difference arises because loans deemed uncollectable are written off in national accounts but kept, fully provisioned, in the IFRS gross portfolio: at end-2025 the national-standard loan book was UZS 35.4 trillion, compared with UZS 41.9 trillion of gross loans under IFRS (CBU, 2026; Ipoteka Bank, 2020–2026).

In every specification the index is lowest in 2023 and highest in 2025, so the qualitative conclusion is robust. Levels, however, depend strongly on the accounting basis: with national-standard asset quality and liquidity ratios the 2025 value is 0.720, close to the high-stability threshold, compared with 0.562 under IFRS. Following the prudence principle, the stricter IFRS-based version is used as the baseline. The benchmark Z* matters mainly for classification into stability bands and should be calibrated to the system-wide distribution of Z-scores.

Because asset quality is the binding constraint, the sensitivity of the index to the asset quality score was examined holding other 2025 values constant (Figure 3). If the Stage 3 ratio fell to 6–10 per cent, the index would rise to 0.600; to 4–6 per cent, to 0.637; and to 2–4 per cent, to 0.675. Each one-notch improvement adds 0.0375 to the index – a reserve that no other component offers.

Comparable BBII values for other banks would require consistent IFRS-based disclosures, which are not yet available for most state banks.

# 5. Discussion

## 5.1. Interpretation

The BBII reconciles the conflicting signals described in the introduction. Higher reported impaired loans after the acquisition do not necessarily indicate deteriorating credit quality; they mainly reflect stricter recognition of legacy problems under group standards. The IMF (2025) notes that NPLs may be under-reported in the Uzbek system, and Moody's reported that system-wide problem loans rose to 7 per cent in 2024 as state banks recognized problem assets (Daryo, 2025). The improvement in capitalization and earnings since 2024 is real and is captured by the index. The integral measure therefore supports a nuanced judgement: stability was eroding before the sale, collapsed during integration and has since recovered beyond its pre-sale level, yet it remains only in the lower part of the satisfactory band. High profitability – an ROE of 28.1 per cent against 12.4 per cent for the system – does not by itself imply high stability.

## 5.2. Policy implications

Three implications follow. First, supervisors can use the BBII as an off-site monitoring and peer-comparison tool, particularly for banks undergoing ownership change, where single ratios are misleading; the index must, however, be computed on a single accounting basis for all banks. Second, privatization policy should anticipate a temporary decline in stability indicators in the first post-acquisition year and avoid interpreting it as failure; conversely, a lack of recovery after two to three years should trigger supervisory review. Third, the long memory of the Z-score shows that unrecognized losses carried into a privatization depress the bank's measured stability for years; an independent asset quality review and the carve-out of problem assets before sale would reduce both the integration-year loss and its lasting effect. For foreign-owned banks, home–host supervisory cooperation and limits on intra-group exposures and dividend distributions during periods of elevated impaired loans address the contagion risks documented for multinational banks (de Haas & van Lelyveld, 2014; BCBS, 2012).

## 5.3. Limitations

The analysis has several limitations. It covers a single bank over seven years, so the results illustrate the method rather than establish general effects. The single-indicator proxies for CAMELS components are coarse, and the management proxy in particular captures efficiency rather than governance quality. The full-period Z-score gives a large weight to a single loss year; rolling-window variants would change levels but not the direction of change. Finally, the index measures current condition and insolvency distance but not forward-looking vulnerabilities; combining it with stress tests would strengthen its use.

# 6. Conclusion

This paper develops an integral bank stability index that combines a CAMELS-type rating with the Z-score and can be computed from public disclosures. Applied to Ipoteka Bank, the first state bank in Uzbekistan sold to a foreign strategic investor, and based on its audited IFRS statements, the index reveals a J-shaped trajectory: stability eroded before the sale, collapsed in the integration year and recovered to its highest level by 2025, a result robust to parameter choices and accounting basis. Asset quality is the main remaining weakness: reducing the Stage 3 ratio below 10 per cent would raise the index to about 0.6. The index offers supervisors and policymakers in emerging markets a transparent tool for monitoring banks through privatization and integration.
